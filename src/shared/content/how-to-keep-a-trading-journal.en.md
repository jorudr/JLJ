## Why keep a trading journal at all

Memory is unreliable, and it is especially unreliable about your own losing trades. A trading journal replaces guesswork with a record: what setup you took, why you took it, what you planned to do, and what you actually did. Over weeks and months, that record is what turns a string of isolated trades into a pattern you can act on.

## 1. Record every trade, not just the interesting ones

Skipping the boring or embarrassing trades defeats the purpose. The small mistakes and the trades that barely worked out are usually where the real patterns live.

## 2. Capture the setup and the reasoning, not just the numbers

Entry price, exit price and P&L tell you what happened. They do not tell you why you took the trade or whether it matched your plan. Add the setup, the market context and the reasoning behind the entry and exit.

## 3. Attach a screenshot

A chart screenshot at the moment of entry captures information that is easy to misremember later — where support and resistance actually were, what the broader trend looked like, how clean or messy the setup really was.

## 4. Tag trades by setup and mistake

Tags turn a list of trades into searchable data. Over time, tags like "breakout," "revenge trade" or "moved stop loss" let you filter your history and see exactly how often a specific pattern shows up and what it costs you.

## 5. Review on a fixed schedule

A journal nobody reviews is just a backlog. Set a weekly or monthly review where you look at tagged patterns, compare strategy versions if you run more than one, and check whether your equity curve reflects genuine progress or short-term variance.

## 6. Compare results against your stated rules

The most useful review question is not "did I win or lose" but "did I follow my own rules." A trade that loses while following the plan is very different from a trade that loses because the plan was abandoned under pressure — and only a journal with recorded rules and context can tell the two apart.
